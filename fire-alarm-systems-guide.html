FIRE ALARM SYSTEMS GUIDE

Conventional vs addressable panels, detector types and maintenance — what building owners should know.

Published October 2026 · By the CNE Zone team · All articles

A fire alarm system has one job: give people enough warning to get out safely. Choosing and maintaining it properly matters far more than most other building systems, because it is the one you hope never to use. This guide explains the main options in plain language.

How a fire alarm system works

Detectors and manual call points send signals to a control panel. When the panel recognises a fire condition, it triggers sounders and strobes, and can also send signals to other systems such as door release, lift recall or building management. A monitoring or notification link can alert responsible people off-site.

Conventional vs addressable

Conventional systems

Devices are wired in zones, and the panel shows which zone is in alarm but not which individual device. They are simple and cost less, so they suit small buildings with few areas.

Addressable systems

Each device has its own address, so the panel shows the exact detector or call point that triggered. Faults such as a dirty detector or a cut cable are also identified precisely. In larger or multi-floor buildings this saves valuable time during an emergency and during maintenance, which is why addressable systems are usually preferred for offices, hospitals, schools and factories.

Choosing the right detector

  • Smoke detectors: best for offices, bedrooms and corridors where smouldering fires produce smoke early.
  • Heat detectors: suited to kitchens, boiler rooms and dusty spaces where smoke detectors would cause false alarms.
  • Multi-sensor detectors: combine smoke and heat sensing to reduce false alarms while staying sensitive.
  • Beam detectors: cover large open areas such as warehouses and atriums.
  • Flame detectors: used in high-risk industrial areas where fast response to open flames is needed.

Putting the wrong detector in the wrong place is one of the commonest causes of nuisance alarms, which in turn teach people to ignore the system.

Zoning and design

A building is divided into zones so responders can see where the fire is. Designers consider occupancy, evacuation routes, ceiling heights and any special hazards. Many designers follow recognised standards such as NFPA 72 or BS 5839, and your insurer or local authority may require compliance with specific codes. Always confirm which rules apply to your building before design starts.

Integration with other systems

A well-integrated alarm can release electric locks on exit doors, shut down ventilation, bring lifts to the ground floor and show alarm locations on a graphic screen. This is where fire alarm and access control design must be coordinated, so that security never blocks escape.

Maintenance matters

Detectors get dirty, batteries age and cables get damaged during renovations. A good maintenance plan includes regular functional testing of detectors, sounders and call points, battery checks on the panel, and a written logbook of tests and faults. Systems that are installed and forgotten are the ones that fail when needed.

Questions to ask your installer

  • Which standard is the design based on?
  • Will I receive drawings, a device schedule and commissioning records?
  • How are false alarms minimised?
  • Who performs periodic testing and how is it recorded?
