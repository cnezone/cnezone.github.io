SECURE YOUR CCTV NETWORK

Default passwords, network segmentation, firmware and remote access — keep your cameras from becoming a security hole.

Published October 2026 · By the CNE Zone team · All articles

An IP camera is a small computer on your network. If it is left on default settings, it can become an entry point for attackers, a source of leaked footage or part of a botnet. The good news is that a handful of basic habits prevent most problems.

1. Change every default password

Many devices ship with well-known default usernames and passwords, and automated scanners try them constantly. Change them on every camera, recorder and switch during installation, and use long, unique passwords rather than reusing one everywhere. Store them in a password manager or a secured record handed to the responsible person.

2. Separate the camera network

Put cameras and recorders on their own VLAN or network segment, apart from staff computers and guest Wi-Fi. If one device is compromised, segmentation limits how far an attacker can move. Allow only the traffic that is needed, such as cameras talking to the recorder and the recorder talking to authorised viewing computers.

3. Do not expose devices directly to the internet

Port-forwarding a recorder to the public internet so you can view it remotely is a common and risky shortcut. Prefer a VPN into the network, or a vendor cloud service that uses encrypted connections and strong authentication. If remote access is essential, restrict it and enable two-step verification where available.

4. Keep firmware updated

Manufacturers publish firmware updates that fix security vulnerabilities. Schedule checks at least a few times a year, test updates on one device first and note the versions in your records. Devices that no longer receive updates should be planned for replacement.

5. Use accounts sensibly

  • Create separate accounts for administrators and viewers.
  • Give staff only the permissions they need.
  • Remove accounts promptly when people leave.
  • Avoid shared logins so actions can be traced to a person.

6. Protect the physical side too

Recorders and switches should sit in a locked cabinet or room. Unused network ports should be disabled or secured, so that nobody can plug a laptop into an open camera port. Time-synchronise devices so footage timestamps are reliable in an investigation.

7. Back up and monitor

Keep a backup of device configurations so a failed recorder can be restored quickly. Monitoring tools that alert you when a camera goes offline also help spot tampering, since a camera that suddenly disappears from the network deserves a closer look.

8. Think about privacy

Cameras should cover areas where monitoring is appropriate, and signage helps visitors understand that recording takes place. Limit who can export footage and keep a record of exports. Good privacy practice also builds trust with staff and customers.
