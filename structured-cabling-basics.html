STRUCTURED CABLING BASICS

Cat6, Cat6A, fiber, distance limits and labeling — the foundation every other system depends on.

Published October 2026 · By the CNE Zone team · All articles

Cameras, access controllers, Wi-Fi access points and phones all depend on the same thing: cabling. Poor cabling causes dropouts that are hard to diagnose, and it is expensive to fix once walls are painted and ceilings closed. Doing it properly once is far cheaper than patching it for years.

What "structured" cabling means

Structured cabling is a planned, standards-based system rather than a collection of cables run wherever was convenient. Cables run from outlets to patch panels in a rack, where they connect to switches through short patch cords. Because everything terminates in one organised place, changes are simple and faults are quick to trace.

Copper categories

  • Cat5e: adequate for basic gigabit networks, but now considered the minimum.
  • Cat6: a common choice for new offices and camera networks, supporting gigabit comfortably and 10 Gigabit over shorter runs.
  • Cat6A: supports 10 Gigabit over the full channel length and has better shielding performance, which suits high-density or future-proof installations.

Whatever category you pick, the cable, connectors and patch panels should all be rated for it; the weakest component sets the performance.

The 100 metre rule

Twisted-pair Ethernet is limited to 100 metres per channel. In practice this means up to 90 metres of permanent cable between outlet and patch panel, plus short patch cords at each end. Cameras mounted far from a building, such as at a gate or perimeter, need either a nearby switch cabinet or a fiber link.

When to use fiber

Fiber optic cable carries data over long distances, is immune to electrical interference and is the right choice for linking buildings or floors. Outdoor links between buildings should use fiber partly because it does not conduct lightning surges the way copper can. Single-mode fiber suits long campus links, while multimode is common for shorter runs inside a building.

Power over Ethernet

PoE sends power to cameras and access points through the data cable. Standard PoE provides around 15 watts per port, PoE+ about 30 watts, and higher-power standards go further for PTZ cameras and heaters. When you design the network, add up the power drawn by all devices and make sure the switch has enough total power budget, not just enough ports.

Labeling and documentation

Every cable should be labeled at both ends and recorded in a simple schedule. Without labels, finding one faulty camera cable among dozens becomes guesswork. As-built drawings and a port list are some of the most valuable things an installer can hand over.

Testing and certification

A professional installation tests every run with a cable certifier, not just a continuity tester. A certifier checks performance against the standard and produces a report for each cable, which protects you if problems appear later.

Plan for growth

Add spare outlets and conduit space while the walls are open. It costs very little during installation and a great deal later. A small rack with room for additional switches and a UPS keeps the network tidy and ready for new cameras or devices.
