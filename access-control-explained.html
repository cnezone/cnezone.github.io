ACCESS CONTROL EXPLAINED

Cards, PINs, biometrics and mobile credentials — how they compare and where each one fits.

Published October 2026 · By the CNE Zone team · All articles

Keys are easy to copy, easy to lose and impossible to audit. Access control replaces them with credentials that can be issued, limited and cancelled in seconds, and every entry is logged. This article explains how these systems work and how to choose the right type for your premises.

The four parts of every access control system

  • Credential: what a person presents — a card, fob, PIN, fingerprint, face or phone.
  • Reader: the device at the door that reads the credential.
  • Controller: the unit that decides whether to open the door, based on rules you set.
  • Lock hardware: an electric strike, magnetic lock or motorised lock that physically secures the door.

Software ties these together, letting you add users, set schedules and review who went where and when.

Comparing credential types

Proximity and smart cards

Cards are cheap, familiar and quick to issue. Basic proximity cards are easy to clone, so newer encrypted smart-card formats are a better choice for anywhere security matters. The weakness of any card is that it can be lent to someone else or lost.

PIN codes

PINs need no hardware to carry, but they are shared easily and shoulder-surfed. They work best as a second factor combined with a card or biometric on sensitive doors.

Fingerprint

Fingerprint readers tie access to a person rather than an object, which makes buddy-punching and card sharing harder. They can struggle with very dry, worn or dirty fingers, so they suit offices better than workshops with heavy manual work.

Face recognition

Face readers are contactless and fast, useful in busy entrances or where hygiene matters. Check the quality of the liveness detection and how the device handles poor lighting before you commit.

Mobile credentials

Phone-based credentials reduce card costs and can be issued remotely. They depend on staff phones and battery life, so many sites keep a card fallback.

Match the door to the risk

Not every door needs the same protection. A reception door may only need a card reader, while a server room or store should use two factors, such as card plus fingerprint. Keep a simple list of doors ranked by risk and assign credentials accordingly.

Fire safety comes first

Locks must release safely during a fire or power failure. Magnetic locks, for example, are normally fail-safe, meaning they unlock when power is cut, and exit routes need a way out that does not depend on a credential. Access control should be designed together with the fire alarm so that alarm activation releases the right doors. This is one of the main reasons to use an installer who also understands life-safety systems.

Time and attendance as a bonus

Because the system already records every entry, it can also feed attendance reports. This removes manual registers and helps payroll, as long as you decide clearly which readers count as attendance points.

Common mistakes

  • Using one master credential for everyone.
  • Not removing access when staff leave.
  • Installing readers without planning for power cuts or a backup battery.
  • Ignoring the cabling route to the door, which is where most installation problems occur.
