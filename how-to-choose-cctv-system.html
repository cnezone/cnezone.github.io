HOW TO CHOOSE A CCTV SYSTEM

IP vs analog, resolution, storage sizing and camera placement — what actually matters before you buy.

Published October 2026 · By the CNE Zone team · All articles

Most CCTV disappointments are not caused by bad cameras. They come from a system that was never matched to the building: cameras pointed at the wrong places, storage that overwrites footage after three days, or night footage that shows nothing but glare. This guide walks through the decisions that matter, in the order you should make them.

1. Start with what you need to see

Before comparing brands or megapixels, write down the purpose of each camera position. Security professionals usually describe four levels of detail: detect (is something there?), observe (what is it doing?), recognise (is this someone I know?) and identify (can this footage support an investigation?). A parking lot overview only needs to detect and observe. A cash counter or main entrance needs to identify. The same building will usually need a mix of all four, and that mix drives how many cameras you need and what lenses they should have.

2. IP cameras vs analog (HD-over-coax)

Analog HD systems reuse existing coaxial cabling and are cheaper for small upgrades. IP cameras send digital video over network cable, which gives you higher resolutions, better analytics, power over the same cable (PoE) and easier expansion. For a new installation, IP is almost always the better long-term choice. For a small shop that already has coax running to every location, analog HD can still be the sensible budget option.

3. Resolution: more is not always better

A 4 MP (2K) camera is a strong default for most indoor and outdoor positions. 8 MP (4K) is useful where you need to cover a wide area and still zoom into detail afterwards, such as a yard or a long corridor. Remember that higher resolution means larger files, so it directly affects storage cost and network load. Choosing the right resolution for each position is better than putting the highest one everywhere.

4. Size your storage before you buy

Storage is the part buyers most often underestimate. A rough formula: bitrate (Mbps) × 86,400 seconds ÷ 8 ÷ 1,000 = GB per camera per day. A 4 MP camera using H.265 compression and recording continuously at around 4 Mbps produces about 43 GB per day. Sixteen such cameras kept for 30 days need roughly 20 TB. Motion-based recording, lower frame rates at quiet hours and efficient compression can reduce this, but it is better to plan for the real number than to discover after an incident that footage was overwritten.

  • Decide the retention period first (14, 30 or 60 days).
  • Use surveillance-grade hard drives designed for continuous writing.
  • Consider RAID or a second recorder for critical sites.

5. Low light, glare and weather

Night performance depends on the sensor, the lens and the infrared range, not just the megapixel number. Cameras facing bright lights or windows need wide dynamic range (WDR) so that both bright and dark areas stay visible. Outdoor units should have a proper ingress-protection rating, such as IP66, to handle dust and heavy rain, and vandal-resistant housings are worth it in reachable positions.

6. Camera placement basics

  • Mount entrance cameras at a height and angle that captures faces, not just the tops of heads.
  • Avoid pointing cameras directly at the sun or at bright lamps.
  • Cover blind spots at corners, stairwells, loading areas and any room holding cash or equipment.
  • Keep cameras out of easy reach, or use vandal-resistant models if that is not possible.

7. Think about the network and power

IP cameras are only as reliable as the cabling and switches behind them. Use quality network cable, a PoE switch with enough power budget for all cameras, and keep the camera network separate from your office network where possible. A small uninterruptible power supply (UPS) on the recorder and switch keeps the system recording through short power cuts.

8. Ask about support before you sign

A good installer should provide a camera layout plan, hand over login details and documentation, and explain how footage is exported when you need it. Ask who you call when a camera goes offline and how quickly they respond. A system that nobody maintains slowly becomes a system that nobody trusts.
